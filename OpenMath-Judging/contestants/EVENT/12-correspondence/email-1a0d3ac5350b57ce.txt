OpenMath: Mathematics and Formal Verification Challenge
September 27–October 2, 2026

OpenMath is a week-long challenge bringing together academic researchers,
students, independent mathematicians, and AI engineers to make verifiable
progress on mathematical problems using human reasoning, AI tools, or a
combination of both.

Dates, location, and format

   - The in-person gathering takes place on Sunday, September 27, from 10
   a.m. to 10 p.m. Eastern at Harvard Innovation Labs. This updates earlier
   invitations that listed MIT CSAIL at noon.
   - The online competition continues through Friday, October 2. The
   submission deadline currently communicated by the organizers is 11:59 p.m.
   Eastern that day.
   - Participation can be entirely remote; attendance at the opening is not
   required.
   - The organizing community includes RSI House, Autolab, and Sundai,
   which grew out of the MIT and Harvard communities.

Mathematical focus and evaluation

The event features six selected open problems, rated by difficulty through
the Ulam Open Problems Difficulty Atlas. Participants explore mathematical
arguments, formalize proofs, and use proof-checker feedback to improve
their submissions.

Results are subject to formal checking and mathematical review. Meaningful
partial progress, formalizations of existing mathematics, and substantive
variations on known results are also relevant contributions.

Any AI tools are permitted, as is human-only work. Autolab provides an
environment for iterative research workflows, but participants can use
their own tools.

Alejandro Zarzuelo’s contributions include developing the difficulty atlas
and the mathematical and evaluation framework. He has also been invited to
serve on the jury.

Expected participants

The organizers estimate approximately 80–120 participants, including
academic researchers, undergraduate and graduate students, independent
mathematicians, and engineers, with anticipated participation from the MIT,
Harvard, Northeastern, and Boston University communities. These are
planning estimates rather than confirmed attendance figures.

The event is open to people with university-level mathematical training,
including backgrounds in mathematics, physics, engineering, and computer
science.

Sunday’s published schedule

All times are Eastern.

   - 10:00 a.m.: AI news
   - 10:15 a.m.: Introduction to recursive self-improvement
   - 10:45 a.m.: Idea pitching and team formation
   - 2:00 p.m.: Progress check-in and lunch
   - 4:00 p.m.: Optional run or bike ride
   - 8:00 p.m.: Presentations of the day’s work

Sunday’s presentations are separate from the final submissions for the
week-long competition. The organizers plan to begin judging on Friday and
hold a results ceremony after reviews are complete.

Research collaboration and compute support

Opportunities for involvement include participation by research teams,
advising participants, reviewing results, and longer-term collaboration on
AI-assisted mathematics and formal verification.

Compute resources and model API credits would support repeated
proof-generation and verification attempts, as well as longer research runs.

Existing sponsorship discussions identified $50 per approved participant as
a useful starting allocation. Across 80–120 participants, this would
correspond to approximately $4,000–$6,000 in credits if everyone received
that allocation. This is an indicative budget, not confirmed funding or a
complete estimate of demand. Extended reasoning runs may require additional
resources.

Proposed distribution options include access through Autolab and individual
API credits for participants working independently. Artem Lukoianov is
coordinating the credit request and distribution. The organizers had
targeted September 24 for initial access to allow testing before the
opening.

Any support conditional on deeper research collaboration or particular
open-source commitments would require agreement on workloads, resource
allocation, public outputs, and applicable terms.
